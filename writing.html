Research Manuscript · DePaul & Indiana University · In Progress

Toward the Design of a Transmasc Sex Ed Tool

Jacob Boss, Mel Monier, Thendral Prabu (Advisor: Patrick Shih)

This project builds on survey findings from 52 transmasculine participants about how they seek sexual education. Participants reported low trust in school curricula and medical practitioners and relied on peers, romantic partners, and online media instead. Few technologies currently provide sexual education that reflects transmasculine bodies and experiences. Building on these findings, this follow-up study conducts an Asynchronous Remote Community (ARC) with 20 transmasculine participants through Microsoft Teams to explore their experiences, perspectives, and design preferences for a sexual education tool. The goal is to inform the design of a platform that combines educational rigor with community perspectives.

Literature Review: How Design Creates Disability

Thendral Prabu

Examining how "empathy" masquerades as accessibility in design

UX Design Theory Accessibility Disability

Abstract

Drawing on research from the fields of human-centered design, disability studies, computer-supported cooperative work, and organizational policy, this literature review explores how social, political, technical and spatial design choices echo in the modern understanding of disability, a construct that others people that fail to fit in universal design’s persona of the ideal average. Environments built for an imagined average marginalize people with chronic illness, neurodivergence, and age-related conditions which further shapes how disability is constructed and experienced through the lens of design. Universities, rooted in historically exclusionary structures, impose rigid curricula and inflexible technologies that neurodivergent students must navigate through student-driven interventions, and corporate workplaces reinforce ableist norms by forcing the continuous productivity at the benefit of profit over person, expectations that minimize the needs of people with invisible conditions and provide exacting conditions designed for managerial supervision that promote sensory and physical strain. Critical literature foregoes these assumptions in favor of person-first designs that encourage consideration of the unpredictability of the human body and the diversity of the sensory experience by building in flexibility in organizational policy and urban design. Literature calls for adaptive, co-designed systems that prioritize rest instead of attacking disabled bodies and condemns the performative practice of empathy that design espouses.

Introduction: Universal Design as a Perpetuator of Able-Bodied Assumptions

Across the fields of human-centered design, architecture, and product design, universal design has been offered as the panacea to exclusion. However, universal design’s crutch of the "average" body creates a contrived persona without cognitive or bodily differences, excluding large groups of people in favor of ease over rigor in design practices. When designers rely on the imagined average of abilities and backgrounds, they smooth out the rich tapestry of human experience that the field has the potential to represent, prioritizing instead personal choice and inevitably reinforcing political, social, and urban norms that presume a baseline of cognitive and physical ability. Chronic illness, a purely medical phenomenon by definition, becomes disability when the norm becomes that of a fabricated average, and the true diversity of human experience is lost to a poorly conceived heuristic.

Literature Review: Environmental Design of Public Spaces

Environmental design of public spaces—parks, workplaces, schools, museums—exposes the invisible ableism of design, the layouts and affordances belying what bodies they were modeled for. The rest and recovery for the average are not the same as that required by those that are differently abled, as Janicki et al. (2025) poses infrastructure as an exclusionary force by purposefully avoiding quiet spaces in these shared spaces. These same systemic assumptions that drive urban design are repeated in research, policy, and technology, as places of work and education assume that the persona they cater to is between the ages of 25 and 50, creating a dearth of scholarly material in the aging population (Fässberg et al., 2015). The functional limitations of design create the most effect in its most understudied population, a group that is also its most vulnerable in health and mental illness concerns: people over the age of 60.

Designers too often pluck from their own lived experiences, reiterating what is known in literature and deepening the divide between the most marginalized populations. They fail to consider non-designers, a decision that has rippling effects into what data is readily available, who design caters for, and how design shapes the world.

How Existing Systems Perpetuate Disability

Exclusion in design begins in institutionalized systems: corporations and universities. These same institutions are the ones producing flawed design thinking perpetuating false understandings of empathy around disabilities and creating technology that serves the general population, creating a degree of separation in those who need "assistive" technology, a term rooted in "ableist rhetoric that implies a distinct category of tools designed exclusively for people with 'impairments' to better function in 'normal' society" (Janicki et al., 2025).

Empathy-driven design perpetuates misunderstandings surrounding disability rather than gathering firsthand primary data. True empathy, as defined by Bennett and Rosner, is "co-feeling" and co-designing with the group you are designing for instead of designing based off perceptions of a disability, in which designers plunge their hands into ice to simulate arthritis or spend the day in a wheelchair to deign to understand mobility issues. These half-measures of understanding disability fail to recreate the complexities of a real person’s life and "do little to reveal nuances of different contexts, experiences over time, and the myriad factors that impact disability including greater cultural, institutional, and social influences," distilling the disabled experience into a commodity to capitalize on the insights (Bennett and Rosner, 2019). The act of recreating a disability not only "fails to account for the diverse coping mechanisms and innovative techniques persons with disabilities develop in long-term situations," but also centers the designer’s short-lived experience rather than that of the person being designed for (Abreu, 2018).

The act of recreating a disability centers the designer’s short-lived experience rather than that of the person being designed for."

Empathy "upholds the workings of colonialism and oppression," furthered by institutional policies upheld by universities and corporations, bodies traditionally understood to prop up wealthy, able-bodied men over marginalized populations (Bennett and Rosner, 2019). In universities specifically, neurodiverse students bear the weight of "access labor—the practices of negotiating and seeking equitable access to organizational services, technologies, and resources"—limited further by issues of siloed knowledge, limitations of assistive technology, and social barriers; expert knowledge on how to cater to students with disabilities remains within the disability services department and is not disseminated to other university professionals, and students with invisible disabilities or those without formal diagnoses face stigma and dismissal due to the categorization of their differences as "special needs" (Borsotti et al., 2024). The rigidity of these systems and the standardized approach to education forces students to mold to exacting measures, leading students to respond by implementing "micro-interventions" as focused measures to succeed. Student-designed interventions highlight how organizational policy has failed them, demonstrating how technology serves as a crucial tool to level the playing field when traditional learning systems are inadequate.

The same issues resurface later in the workspace, particularly in open-plan offices. The open office presents a cheap solution to supporting collaboration and efficiency, serving only the corporate bottom line. The bodily response to acute white noise—a byproduct of the lack of privacy in office settings—serves to decrease motivation and increase perceived stress (Evans and Johnson, 2000). People living with chronic illnesses—migraines, fibromyalgia, Lyme disease—are especially sensitive to noise-related stressors and prone to flareups from prolonged exposure, giving way to the illusion that people with chronic conditions are liabilities in the workplace, forcing them to either conceal symptoms or disclose them at the risk of appearing incompetent. Ganesh and Lazar (2021) emphasize the need for a flexible workplace that allows for rest over mere tool-based accommodations, as fostering a rest-positive space encourages all people regardless of disability status to maximize their potential away from the punishing capitalist expectation of continuous output over wellness.

Spatial Design and Productivity

Shared spaces of work and education hold the clearest examples of how the social dichotomy of able and disabled is perpetuated in architectural design. The modern office prioritizes harsh, colorless rows that allow for managerial oversight, aesthetic minimalism, and corporate efficiency at the cost of human comfort and health. Corporate policy demands unmoving attention from the human brain for eight hours—despite research indicating capacity for only three to five hours of deep focus—and a body that demands movement at least every thirty minutes to avoid sedentary-related illnesses.

Reconceptualizing rest as a crucial part of the day decenters the able-bodied narrative in organizational policy and supports people with chronic illnesses who require low-stimulus restorative spaces, destigmatizing fatigue by providing equal access to counteract unpredictable flareups. Reducing artificial scents, increasing access to natural light, controlling acoustic pollution, and building designated quiet zones in workspaces not only benefits those with chronic illnesses, but also people with cognitive differences such as autism and ADHD, ensuring spatial design supports natural variability rather than punishing difference (Dobstaff, 2024). Building places of reprieve in spatial design offsets the social burden placed on people with disabilities, avoiding the constant justification many face simply to fulfill basic physiological needs.

Conclusion: Implications of Design as a Driving Force for Disability in HCI

By acknowledging the social and environmental factors that constitute disability, designers can dismantle reductive understandings that bleed into misguided attempts at empathy. Chronic illness and mental differences become disability when repeatedly ignored by design through technology, methodology, and policy that determine the narrow abstraction of a "normal" human.

Abolishing the ill-fated persona that drives universal design shapes an equitable design ecosystem in which cognitive and bodily function do not dictate prerequisite user status, reducing harm perpetuated by inadequate datasets and deficit-based views of disability. Disability arises when systems fail to meet people where they are (Rioux and Daly, 2010). As designers, the goal is to eliminate disability by embracing co-design, the first step in abolishing the rigid systems that determine the "other." Janicki et al. (2024) observed the parallel between chronic illness and plant life in their machine-defying entropy, captured only by rich, human-first research that HCI promises. Our goal is to shift away from the universal design singularity and dismantle the narrative of the average person to better serve the individual, breaking away from the rigid systems that yield disability.
